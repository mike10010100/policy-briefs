**ISSUE BRIEF**
Series: Practical AI Governance Framework (Brief 2026-01D)
Prepared by: Michael Paulauski
Date: November 15, 2026
Subject: Worker tax parity, workplace AI rules, and displacement insurance

### Bottom line up front

Pew Research finds that 71% of Americans expect AI to reduce total U.S. jobs over the next two decades, with concern among adults under 30 jumping from 61% in 2024 to 73% in 2026. Yet current U.S. tax law tilts employers toward replacing workers even when automation barely breaks even, while outdated WARN thresholds and expired Trade Adjustment Assistance leave displaced staff without transition income.

Instead of unworkable 'robot taxes' or low-return classroom vouchers, federal policy should fix the capital-vs-labor tax wedge, set hard workplace dignity and bargaining rights, and build automatic wage insurance and paid sectoral career paths.

### 1. Fix the bonus-depreciation wedge without penalizing open-weight software

Research by Daron Acemoglu, Andrea Manera, and Pascual Restrepo in *Brookings Papers on Economic Activity* calculates that U.S. tax law imposes a 25.5% to 33.5% effective tax rate on human labor (combining income and payroll taxes, and reaching 33.5% with benefit-phaseout taxes) while taxing software and equipment at roughly 5%, a gap locked back in when the 2025 tax law restored permanent 100% first-year bonus depreciation and immediate domestic software write-offs. That wedge subsidizes "so-so automation" and pushes firms to replace staff with mediocre software largely for the tax break.

Congress should scale back corporate 100% bonus depreciation toward economic depreciation while carefully preserving domestic R&D and software development expensing under Section 174A (so local open-weight deployments are not taxed worse than cloud APIs deducted under Section 162), alongside small-business Section 179 hardware limits, grid hardware, domestic chip fabs, and restored clean-energy tax incentives cut back in July 2025.

Congress should pair the resulting corporate tax revenues with a refundable working-class payroll tax credit backed by a mandatory dollar-for-dollar general-revenue transfer that keeps the Social Security and Medicare Trust Funds completely whole.

### 2. Outlaw abusive "bossware," forced arbitration, and secret deployments

On the job, federal labor law should require an accountable human decision-maker for hiring, firing, pay, and work-speed safety, outlaw injury-inducing algorithmic quotas ("bossware"), and bar forced pre-dispute arbitration waivers over workplace AI violations so non-union workers can actually enforce statutory protections.

Consistent with the AFL-CIO's Workers First Initiative on AI, Congress should guarantee workers advance notice, vendor audit disclosures, and anti-retaliation protections before covered employers introduce workplace surveillance, quota, or mass-displacement AI.

Under the National Labor Relations Act, Congress should make workplace surveillance and automated quotas a mandatory subject of pre-deployment collective bargaining alongside mandatory effects bargaining over severance, scheduling, and transition protections.

### 3. Pair federal wage insurance and modernized WARN with paid sectoral training

Tax parity only stops marginal automation; where capable AI agents outperform humans at a fraction of payroll cost, mid-career cognitive workers will still face displacement where legacy retraining programs like the expired Trade Adjustment Assistance system and generic WIOA classroom vouchers failed. Building on Hamilton Project and Brookings models, Congress should enact federal wage insurance for displaced workers with at least one year of job tenure that temporarily replaces half of the earnings drop (up to an inflation-indexed annual cap such as $15,000 to $20,000 for two years) when they take a lower-paying job with a new employer.

Congress should also update the federal WARN Act so distributed remote and rolling mass layoffs trigger 90-day notice and mandatory severance transition funding without reducing state unemployment benefits.

For skill transitions and younger entrants, federal workforce funds should shift away from generic classroom vouchers toward paid apprenticeships and employer-backed sectoral training in shortage fields like nursing, grid trades, and public-interest tech (high-performing sectoral models shown to raise multi-year earnings in MDRC randomized trials, led over ten years by St. Nicks Alliance), paired with broader Federal Unemployment Tax Act training-approval standards and extended UI stipends so displaced workers keep income support during full-time programs. Finally, Congress should modernize ERISA multi-employer plan rules so unions and sectoral boards can run portable benefit pools across short-tenure jobs without weakening W-2 misclassification laws.

### Trade-offs to weigh in policy design

**04 · Tax Parity & Reemployment vs. Robot Taxes**
Tax economists across the spectrum warn that literal "robot taxes" or domestic AI compute taxes are nearly impossible to define in tax law and risk pushing data centers abroad, while MDRC implementation studies caution that high-performing sectoral training programs are hard to scale rapidly without deep employer commitment. Pairing general bonus-depreciation reform with trust-fund-neutral payroll credits and rapid-reemployment wage insurance cushions displaced workers immediately without taxing domestic compute or stranding laid-off staff in low-return classes.

### Appendix: Plain-English technical and policy glossary

#### Business tax rules

**100% bonus depreciation vs. economic depreciation ("so-so automation")**
Bonus depreciation (IRC Section 168(k)) lets businesses deduct the full cost of new and eligible used equipment and purchased software in Year 1 instead of spreading deductions over the asset's useful working life (economic depreciation), while Section 174A covers domestic R&D and cloud API subscriptions are deducted as operating expenses under Section 162. Economists use "so-so automation" to describe mediocre systems that barely match human staff and get deployed mainly because capital write-offs are taxed far more lightly than human wages.

#### Workplace law and income transitions

**"Bossware," wage insurance, and sectoral training**
"Bossware" is workplace software that monitors keystrokes, cameras, or movement speed to enforce rigid quotas or automated discipline. Wage insurance is a temporary federal paycheck supplement (such as covering half of a pay drop up to an indexed annual cap for two years) when a laid-off worker starts a new job at lower pay. Sectoral training pairs public workforce funds directly with employers and unions to train and hire workers for specific job shortages.

**WARN Act, FUTA, and ERISA multi-employer plans**
The federal WARN Act currently requires employers with 100 or more full-time workers to give 60 days' notice before plant closings affecting 50 or more workers, or single-site mass layoffs affecting at least 500 workers (or 50 to 499 workers making up at least one-third of active site staff). FUTA (the Federal Unemployment Tax Act) sets federal baseline rules for state unemployment insurance. ERISA governs private workplace health and retirement benefits, though its portable multi-employer benefit pools are currently limited largely to union collective-bargaining agreements.
