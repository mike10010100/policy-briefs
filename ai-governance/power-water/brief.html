Subject:Clean power, water, and air rules for hyperscale data centers

Bottom line up front

Across the PJM grid spanning 13 states and Washington, D.C., PJM's Independent Market Monitor calculates that existing and forecast data-center load added $9.33 billion to the 2025/2026 wholesale capacity auction, while U.S. Department of Energy resource-adequacy and FERC RM26-4 large-load filings warn that rapid load growth is colliding with plant retirements and driving up household electric bills. Meanwhile, single dry-basin campuses pull millions of gallons of municipal water daily or run trailer-mounted gas turbines outside major-source air permits.

Federal and state policy should enforce strict cost causation: require large facilities to fund grid upgrades up front, unlock clean interconnection queues through modest peak flexibility and surplus-capacity reuse, condition federal water and energy aid on zero- or low-evaporation cooling and drought protections, and close data-center turbine air loopholes.

1. Require 100% cost-causation tariffs with minimum-term collateral

Through FERC wholesale transmission and capacity rules, Department of Energy grid-grant conditions, and state public utility commission rate standards, policymakers should require 100% cost-causation tariffs paired with minimum-term financial collateral for hyperscale loads (such as facilities or campuses over 20 megawatts, aggregated across phased buildings, co-located tenants, and behind-the-meter pairings).

Mandatory collateral ensures residential customers and small businesses never subsidize Big Tech substation buildouts or inherit stranded grid debt if a speculative project cancels, consolidates workloads elsewhere, or misses load forecasts.

2. Pair clean-power interconnection priority with peak load curtailment

Congress should empower FERC, ERCOT/non-RTO regulators, and state utility commissions to reserve fast-track interconnection queues for large facilities that bring new, deliverable clean generation or interim clean-capacity bridge contracts on a rising hourly-matched schedule (using near-term solar and battery storage while scaling enhanced geothermal and nuclear) and agree to trim non-urgent runs, shift regional workloads, or draw clean on-site storage without running diesel or fossil-methane backup by 0.25% to 1% of annual electricity use (mostly partial drawdowns across roughly 85 to 366 peak grid-stress hours a year, equal to 22 to 88 full-load hours).

Companion 2025 and 2026 studies from Duke University's Nicholas Institute find that curtailing grid withdrawals during those peak hours unlocks 76 to 126 gigawatts of system-wide headroom across major U.S. balancing authorities (where local transmission can deliver it, achieved via workload shifting or clean storage instead of the fossil backup generators modeled in baseline cases), while shifting deferrable workloads across up to 50% of annual operating hours cuts U.S. power-system capital, operating, and fuel costs by $40 billion to $150 billion over ten years (enforced via utility-metered peak curtailment telemetry and collateral clawbacks).

Congress should also authorize FERC to speed up advanced transmission reconductoring and dynamic line ratings on existing corridors, and prioritize non-emitting generation and storage interconnections that reuse spare hookup capacity at operating plants under Order 845 or retiring fossil sites under a nationwide generator-replacement rule opened to third-party clean projects if incumbents delay.

3. Condition federal water funds and close methane-turbine air loopholes

On water, Water Foundation and Brookings analyses show that a single hyperscale facility can draw 1 million to 5 million gallons of treated water a day in dry basins. Because federal agencies cannot set municipal water rates directly, Congress should condition future EPA State Revolving Fund and Bureau of Reclamation water-project grants, while the executive branch conditions discretionary DOE loans and facilities serving federal cloud contracts, on ending subsidized bulk potable water discounts for large data centers, paired with state drought permitting and industrial groundwater caps.

Those conditions should require zero-evaporation closed-loop, dry-rated hybrid adiabatic, or return-flow-safe reclaimed municipal wastewater cooling in water-stressed basins, with automatic drought shutoffs on any potable draw.

On air pollution, Congress and the EPA should close loopholes for turbines powering fixed data centers by repealing the 2026 Subpart KKKKa 24-month temporary-turbine (25 ppm NOx) and <=45% capacity-factor SCR exemptions while requiring SCR-grade controls on aggregated <=850 MMBtu/h modular turbine banks, blocking EPA's proposed Nonroad Combustion Turbines Rule (RIN 2060-AX08) and barring nonroad-engine claims, aggregating co-located turbines across PSD, Title V, and NSPS limits, codifying Michigan v. DOE against Federal Power Act Section 202(c) retirement overrides and non-blackout 50-hour backup-generator waivers, and statutorily barring Clean Air Act Section 110(f) and Section 112(i)(4) waivers for commercial data centers or fossil plants dedicated to serving them. That ends the unpermitted turbine and emergency fossil practices documented in NAACP, Southern Environmental Law Center, and Earthjustice filings in South Memphis, Southaven, Mississippi, and federal grid dockets.

Trade-offs to weigh in policy design

01 · Permitting Scope & Community Safeguards

Fast-tracking clean energy infrastructure can fracture center-left coalitions if permitting bills sweep too broadly, as Time documented during the 2025 and 2026 permitting battles. Statutory fast-tracks must stay tightly scoped to interregional transmission timelines, reconductoring, dynamic line ratings, geothermal exploration, and battery or zero-carbon generation on low-conflict or remediated sites, while preserving Tribal consultation and cumulative fenceline safeguards everywhere and never granting fast-tracks or weaker NEPA and Clean Air Act enforcement to fossil projects, as demanded by the Sierra Club and frontline community groups.

Appendix: Plain-English technical and policy glossary

8 plain-language definitions for statutory, technical, and institutional terms used in Brief 2026-01A.

PJM grid and wholesale capacity auctions
PJM Interconnection is America's largest regional grid operator, managing wholesale electricity across 13 Mid-Atlantic and Midwest states plus Washington, D.C. Its capacity auctions pay power plants in advance to guarantee enough supply during future peak demand; existing and forecast data-center demand has driven those auction prices sharply higher on household bills.
FERC, state utility commissions, and 100% cost-causation tariffs
FERC (the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission) regulates interstate electricity transmission and wholesale power markets, while state public utility commissions set retail electricity rates. A 100% cost-causation tariff is a wholesale and retail rate rule requiring a giant new electricity user, such as a hyperscale data center, to pay the full cost of the grid upgrades and dedicated supply capacity it triggers so wholesale clearing prices and residential bills do not rise.
Interconnection queues, Order 845, and generator replacement
The interconnection queue is the multi-year engineering waitlist new power projects and large loads face before plugging into transmission or distribution grids. FERC Order 845 ("surplus interconnection") lets new clean generation or batteries share unused injection capacity at an operating power plant, while generator-replacement rules let new projects reuse the existing grid hookup when an older plant retires; both are currently fuel-neutral rules that Congress and FERC can pair with clean-power priority and a nationwide competitive replacement standard at operating or retiring fossil sites.
Peak load curtailment
A binding grid agreement where a data center trims non-urgent computing jobs, shifts regional workloads, or draws clean on-site battery storage (without running diesel or fossil-methane backup generators) during the grid's highest-stress summer and winter hours so utilities do not have to build billions of dollars in extra fossil-fuel peaker plants.
Advanced reconductoring and dynamic line ratings
Advanced reconductoring replaces old steel-core wires on existing transmission towers with modern composite-core cables that carry roughly twice as much electricity without multi-year land fights for new towers. Dynamic line ratings use real-time wind and temperature sensors so grid operators can safely send more power through existing wires on cool or breezy days.
Enhanced geothermal
Clean baseload energy that uses modern directional rock-drilling techniques to tap natural heat miles underground and generate steady 24/7 carbon-free electricity with a small land footprint under strict induced-seismicity and groundwater-casing rules.
Closed-loop, hybrid adiabatic, and reclaimed-water cooling
Closed-loop chillers recirculate fluid in sealed heat exchangers with zero evaporative water loss (paired with on-site storage so higher hot-afternoon power draw does not strain peak grids), while dry-rated hybrid adiabatic systems run without water most of the year and shut off potable misting during droughts. Reclaimed (recycled) water is treated municipal wastewater piped to industrial campuses for non-potable cooling without depleting downstream return flows or local drinking water.
Methane-turbine permitting loopholes (Subpart KKKKa and nonroad claims)
Fixed data centers have avoided Title I major-source PSD controls and SCR limits by claiming Clean Air Act nonroad-engine status meant for movable machinery (which EPA's RIN 2060-AX08 proposal would widen) or using EPA's January 2026 Subpart KKKKa NSPS rule (91 FR 1910), which exempts 24-month temporary units, large turbines at <=45% capacity factor, and all modular turbines <=850 MMBtu/h from SCR controls.
