Bottom line up front

Leaving frontier AI in the hands of four or five cloud conglomerates concentrates too much economic and political power while stifling independent researchers, hospitals, and startups. At the same time, Stanford AI Index, CSIS, and Lowy Institute tracking shows that despite U.S. chip export restrictions, Chinese labs such as DeepSeek and Alibaba have narrowed frontier benchmark gaps to roughly 2.7% through open-weight software releases.

Federal competition policy should expand public compute infrastructure, keep open-weight AI legal to deploy on local hardware, police Big Tech cloud-compute tying and disguised mergers, and outlaw shared pricing algorithms that coordinate rents and consumer prices.

Section 01 · Public Infrastructure & Open Weights

1. Fund permanent NAIRR public compute and keep open-weight models legal

Building on the National Science Foundation's September 2026 $35 million launch of the NAIRR Operations Center (in partnership with the Department of Energy), Congress should pass the bipartisan CREATE AI Act and appropriate multi-billion-dollar funding for publicly owned supercomputing and zero-egress, vendor-neutral cloud vouchers at national labs and university centers.

Direct public funding guarantees that academic researchers, teaching hospitals, civic institutions, and independent startups receive real pre-competitive R&D, open-science training, and public-interest model validation capacity without relying on revocable, lock-in Big Tech cloud credits.

Federal policy should also keep open-weight AI models below Pillar 6's catastrophic bio-cyber tripwires legal so smaller firms, regional hospitals, and municipal institutions can inspect and run AI agents on local or domestic servers without paying permanent rent or ceding sensitive operational data to closed corporate APIs.

Section 02 · Competition Enforcement & Price-Fixing

2. Police cloud tying, disguised acqui-hires, and algorithmic collusion

On antitrust, the FTC and DOJ should block exclusive cloud-compute tying agreements and discriminatory accelerator allocation bottlenecks, while Congress modernizes the Hart-Scott-Rodino Act (15 U.S.C. Section 18a, following the February 2026 judicial vacatur of expanded HSR rules in Chamber of Commerce v. FTC) so dominant-platform "acqui-hire" deals pairing talent buyouts with exclusive or quasi-exclusive IP licenses face mandatory premerger review.

Closing the 12-month aged-data training loophole left in DOJ's May 2026 RealPage consent judgment (and building on state statutes like California's AB 325), Congress should pass a targeted Preventing Algorithmic Collusion Act to outlaw shared pricing algorithms that pool or train on current or historical nonpublic competitor data or coordinate competitor pricing across rival firms.

To avoid interfering with ordinary business analytics, the statute should explicitly spare proprietary or non-pooled single-merchant pricing and inventory software that uses only a firm's own operational data and public market prices without cross-competitor price coordination, keeping enforcement squarely aimed at competing landlords and corporations inflating rents and consumer prices in lockstep.

Trade-offs to weigh in policy design

02 · Open Innovation vs. Oligopoly Capture

Restrictive model-licensing schemes sold under the banner of safety tend to entrench the largest cloud incumbents while pushing open development toward foreign labs. Pairing permanent public compute under NAIRR with legal open-weight distribution preserves domestic competition and lowers market-entry costs, while pairing narrow frontier catastrophic tripwires (covered in Brief 2026-01F) with antitrust enforcement targets actual concentration and collusion risks instead of open code.

Models and public compute

AI agents ("agentic" AI)
AI agents are software systems that go beyond answering single chat prompts to plan and execute multi-step tasks across external software tools, databases, and enterprise workflows, such as organizing hospital records, writing code, or triaging customer tickets, where unverified actions can compound across connected systems.
Open-weight models vs. closed APIs
An open-weight model releases its trained numerical parameters ("weights") publicly so universities, hospitals, and smaller firms can download and run the software on their own servers or local hardware. Closed APIs keep the model locked on a tech company's servers and charge customers ongoing fees for access.
NAIRR public compute and the CREATE AI Act
NAIRR (the National Artificial Intelligence Research Resource) is an NSF-led federal public-compute initiative (with Department of Energy national-lab participation) that launched its $35 million Operations Center in September 2026; the bipartisan CREATE AI Act permanently authorizes NAIRR and scales publicly owned supercomputing and vendor-neutral cloud resources for academic, nonprofit, civic, and startup R&D.

Antitrust and pricing rules

"Acqui-hires" and HSR premerger review
A deal structure (often called a reverse acqui-hire) where a tech giant hires away a startup's founders and core engineering team while taking an exclusive or quasi-exclusive IP license to gain effective control without triggering standard Hart-Scott-Rodino (15 U.S.C. Section 18a) antitrust premerger review.
Algorithmic collusion vs. independent pricing tools
Algorithmic collusion occurs when competing landlords or businesses feed or train shared third-party pricing software on current or historical nonpublic pricing, occupancy, or supply data to coordinate prices above competitive levels. Independent single-merchant tools analyze only public market prices and a firm's own internal operational data.
