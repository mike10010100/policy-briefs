Issue Brief · Brief 2026-01B
Series:Practical AI Governance Framework
Prepared by:Michael Paulauski
Date:November 15, 2026
Subject:Public compute, open-weight models, and algorithmic antitrust

Bottom line up front

Leaving frontier AI in the hands of four or five cloud conglomerates concentrates too much economic and political power while stifling independent researchers, hospitals, and startups. At the same time, Stanford AI Index, CSIS, and Lowy Institute tracking shows that despite U.S. chip export restrictions, top U.S.-China frontier gaps narrowed to 2.7% on LMArena by March 2026 while open-weight releases from labs such as DeepSeek and Alibaba sit within 3.3% of closed leaders and drive wide global adoption.

Federal competition policy should expand public compute infrastructure, protect open-weight AI deployment on local hardware below catastrophic bio-cyber boundaries, police Big Tech cloud-compute tying and disguised mergers, and outlaw shared pricing algorithms that coordinate rents and consumer prices.

1. Fund permanent NAIRR public compute and keep open-weight models legal

Building on the National Science Foundation's September 2026 $35 million launch of the NAIRR Operations Center (coordinating interagency and Department of Energy partner labs), Congress should pass the bipartisan CREATE AI Act and appropriate the full $2.6 billion six-year NAIRR Task Force target for publicly owned supercomputing at national labs and universities alongside vendor-neutral commercial cloud vouchers conditioned on zero operational egress fees and workload portability.

Direct public appropriations (beyond H.R. 2385 and S. 4441's reliance on private cloud donations) guarantee that academic researchers, teaching hospitals, civic institutions, and independent startups receive durable pre-competitive R&D, open-science training, and public-interest validation capacity without relying on revocable, lock-in Big Tech cloud credits.

Federal policy should also protect open-weight AI distribution below Pillar 6's narrow catastrophic bio-cyber tripwires (Brief 2026-01F) from compute licensing bans, paired with NSTM-6 gene-synthesis screening and cyber-hardening, so smaller firms, regional hospitals, and municipal institutions can inspect and run AI agents on local or domestic servers without paying permanent rent or ceding sensitive data to closed APIs.

2. Police cloud tying, disguised acqui-hires, and algorithmic collusion

On antitrust, the FTC and DOJ should block dominant-provider cloud-compute tying agreements and discriminatory accelerator allocation bottlenecks, while Congress modernizes the Hart-Scott-Rodino Act (15 U.S.C. Section 18a, beyond the legacy filing rules restored after the February 2026 vacatur in Chamber of Commerce v. FTC) so dominant-platform "acqui-hire" deals pairing mass technical-talent absorption and investor payouts with exclusive or non-exclusive IP licenses or minority stakes trigger mandatory premerger review on combined deal value.

Closing the 12-month aged-data training loophole left in DOJ's May 2026 RealPage consent judgment (and complementing broader state statutes like California's AB 325), Congress should pass a targeted Preventing Algorithmic Collusion Act (modeled on S. 232) to outlaw shared pricing algorithms that pool or train model weights on current or historical nonpublic competitor data, or facilitate agreement or coercion to align competitor prices across rival firms.

To avoid interfering with lawful business analytics or commercial speech (RealPage, Inc. v. James), the statute should explicitly spare in-house or strictly tenant-isolated single-merchant pricing and inventory software whose model weights and outputs rely solely on a firm's own operational data and public market prices without cross-competitor price coordination.

Trade-offs to weigh in policy design

02 · Open Innovation vs. Oligopoly Capture

Restrictive model-licensing schemes sold under the banner of safety tend to entrench the largest cloud incumbents while pushing open development toward foreign labs. Pairing appropriated public compute under NAIRR with licensing-free open-weight distribution below narrow Pillar 6 catastrophic tripwires (paired with NSTM-6 DNA screening and cyber-hardening) preserves domestic competition and targets real concentration and collusion risks instead of open code.

Appendix: Plain-English technical and policy glossary

5 plain-language definitions for statutory, technical, and institutional terms used in Brief 2026-01B.

AI agents ("agentic" AI)
AI agents are software systems that go beyond answering single chat prompts to plan and execute multi-step tasks across external software tools, databases, and enterprise workflows, such as organizing hospital records, writing code, or triaging customer tickets, where unverified actions can compound across connected systems.
Open-weight models vs. closed APIs
An open-weight model releases its trained numerical parameters ("weights") publicly so universities, hospitals, and smaller firms can download and run the software on their own servers or local hardware, subject to Pillar 6's narrow catastrophic bio-cyber boundaries. Closed APIs keep the model locked on a tech company's servers and charge customers ongoing fees for access.
NAIRR public compute and the CREATE AI Act
NAIRR (the National Artificial Intelligence Research Resource) is an NSF-led federal public-compute initiative (coordinating interagency and DOE partner labs) that launched its $35 million Operations Center in September 2026; the bipartisan CREATE AI Act permanently authorizes NAIRR, requiring dedicated multi-year appropriations (targeting the $2.6 billion Task Force benchmark) beyond voluntary private cloud donations.
"Acqui-hires" and HSR premerger review
A deal structure (often called a reverse acqui-hire) where a dominant platform hires away a startup's founders and core technical team while using exclusive or non-exclusive IP licenses, minority stakes, and investor payoffs to absorb effective control without triggering Hart-Scott-Rodino (15 U.S.C. Section 18a) premerger reporting thresholds.
Algorithmic collusion vs. independent pricing tools
Algorithmic collusion occurs when competing landlords or businesses feed or train shared third-party pricing software or pre-trained weights on current or historical nonpublic pricing, occupancy, or supply data, or use shared software to facilitate price alignment or coercion. Independent in-house or strictly tenant-isolated tools analyze only public market prices and a firm's own internal data without cross-competitor alignment.
